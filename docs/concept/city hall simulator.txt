1. What the gameplay would look like
I'd structure it around a third-person, movement-based tycoon game similar to My Mini Mart. The player starts as the only employee in a small municipal office.









1. Receive citizens and collect applications

Citizens arrive carrying recognizable objects or displaying a request icon. You collect their application folders and bring them to the correct intake desk. Initially, you handle everything yourself.











2. Process documents through physical workstations

Drop files into an intake tray. A clerk or machine processes them, then places them in an output tray. Some cases need an extra step, such as a photo, a payment, or a manual review.











3. Deliver completed work

Carry the finished documents to the approval desk, printer, or collection counter. The citizen receives the result, and the city earns revenue or an operating budget.











4. Expand and automate

Buy more desks, hire clerks, install faster machines, and open new departments. Eventually, workers handle routine jobs while you focus on upgrades, bottlenecks, and exceptional cases.



The important thing is that the player should always have something useful to do, but should gradually move from performing every task to managing the whole operation.
2. Making each job instantly recognizable
I'd give every department a visual language made up of five elements:
1. A dedicated colour.
2. A large, distinctive icon.
3. A recognizable document or physical object.
4. A unique workstation or machine.
5. A recognizable final product.
The player should be able to recognize the job without reading a single word.
3. Example: six departments









Business Licences
Orange

Icon: storefront
- Incoming: A folder containing a storefront symbol.
- Workstation: A business registration desk with a computer and document scanner.
- Process: Verify the business details, approve the licence, print a certificate.
- Output: A framed business licence or a certificate with a shop symbol.
The visual association is always a shop, a business, or a certificate.












Vehicle Registration
Green

Icon: car
- Incoming: A miniature car token attached to a registration folder.
- Workstation: A registration desk with a plate printer.
- Process: Verify ownership, register the vehicle, issue plates.
- Output: A number plate and a small car-registration certificate.
The number plate becomes the signature object. You can even have a tiny vehicle drive away when the service is complete.












Tax Services
Red

Icon: coin stack
- Incoming: A tax folder with a coin symbol.
- Workstation: A calculator desk with a payment terminal.
- Process: Calculate the amount due, check the return, record payment or refund.
- Output: A payment receipt, a coin token, or a refund envelope.
The coins and calculator make this look different from document-heavy departments.












Building Permits
Purple

Icon: house and blueprint
- Incoming: Rolled-up building plans.
- Workstation: A large plan-review table.
- Process: Inspect the application, review the plans, approve or return them.
- Output: A stamped blueprint and a permit sign.
The large blueprints make the work visually distinct, even from a distance.












General Citizen Services
Blue

Icon: person silhouette
- Incoming: An identity-card folder.
- Workstation: A photo booth and identity-document printer.
- Process: Verify identity, update records, print certificates.
- Output: An ID card or official certificate.












Housing and Utilities
Teal

Icon: house with roof
- Incoming: A house-shaped request marker with a utility-bill folder.
- Workstation: A benefits-review desk.
- Process: Check eligibility, approve support, issue a payment or service confirmation.
- Output: A benefit approval letter or a utility-assistance voucher.



These visual themes should appear consistently on the citizen's request bubble, the folder, the workstation, the department sign, and the final output. Colour alone shouldn't carry the distinction, since colours can be difficult to distinguish on small screens.
4. The trick: different jobs, shared production mechanics
You don't actually need six completely different gameplay systems. That would make development unnecessarily expensive.

Instead, use a common workflow with a few distinctive actions per department.
Department	Common steps	Signature action
Business licences	Intake → review → approval → output	Stamp a licence
Vehicle registration	Intake → review → approval → output	Print a number plate
Tax services	Intake → calculation → approval → output	Calculate and collect payment
Building permits	Intake → review → approval → output	Review a blueprint
Citizen services	Intake → identity check → approval → output	Take a photo and print an ID
Housing support	Intake → eligibility check → approval → output	Issue a benefit voucher

This gives you a manageable set of mechanics that can be reused across the game. The different animations, objects, machines, and decisions make each department feel distinct.

I'd also make the workflow visible in the environment. A vehicle-registration station might have a stack of blank plates, a plate printer, and a rack for completed registrations. The tax desk might have calculators, coin trays, and a receipt printer.
5. How to show the player what to do next
At the beginning, a floating icon above the next required workstation should be enough. Later, when multiple departments are operating, use a small task panel.

Illustrative UI concept. The progress bars show workload or completion, not the actual state of a running game.

Tapping a department should highlight its stations and show the next action. In the 3D world, a matching icon appears above the relevant desk. That way, the panel is useful for management without turning the game into a menu simulator.
6. How I'd introduce complexity over time
Don't open with six departments. That would create exactly the confusion you're concerned about.
- Level 1: Citizen services only. Teach collecting folders, delivering them, and getting completed documents back to citizens.
- Level 2: Add tax services. Introduce payments and refunds.
- Level 3: Add business licences. Introduce the licence stamp and business-specific requests.
- Level 4: Add vehicle registration. Introduce a new physical output, the number plate.
- Level 5: Add building permits. Introduce larger documents and a more involved review process.
- Later: Unlock specialist departments, multiple floors, interdepartmental referrals, and automatic document routing.
My recommendation is to make the finished output as recognizable as the incoming request. Players should see a number plate, a stamped blueprint, or a tax receipt and immediately know what they've accomplished.

That gives each job its own identity without requiring you to invent a separate game mechanic for every government service.
