Customs Authority Tycoon: Game plan
I'd build this around a simple fantasy: start as one customs officer processing a few parcels, then grow into a massive international border terminal handling trucks, cargo ships, and aircraft.

The advantage over City Hall Simulator is that customs naturally provides physical objects to move. Parcels, crates, pallets, luggage, shipping containers, and vehicles all look different. That makes the gameplay easier to read, gives the player satisfying physical tasks, and creates opportunities for machinery and automation.

The core loop would combine My Mini Mart-style movement with logistics, inspection decisions, and tycoon progression.
1. The core gameplay loop









1. Receive incoming cargo

Parcels arrive on a conveyor, luggage carts bring bags from an airport, or trucks pull into the terminal. Each shipment has a visible declaration icon.











2. Sort and route shipments

Pick up parcels and carry them to the right station: express clearance, standard inspection, document review, or the scanner. Early on, you do this yourself.











3. Inspect and process

Scanners reveal cargo contents. Match them against declarations, identify suspicious shipments, calculate duties, and send shipments for additional inspection when needed.











4. Clear cargo and earn revenue

Approved shipments receive a green clearance tag and move to the exit. Duties, service fees, and performance rewards fund the next upgrade.











5. Upgrade and automate

Add scanners, inspection bays, sorting belts, warehouses, and staff. Eventually, routine cargo moves through the terminal automatically while you manage exceptions and expansion.



2. How to make different customs jobs recognizable
The same concern you had with City Hall applies here, but customs has an advantage: the cargo itself can communicate what kind of work needs doing.

I'd use a consistent combination of colour, icon, physical cargo, inspection equipment, and final status. Here are seven potential cargo categories.









General cargo

Blue · Box icon

Ordinary parcels, crates, and pallets. The introductory workflow: check declaration, scan, clear, and send to dispatch.












Vehicles

Green · Car icon

Cars, vans, and trucks. Drive them into inspection bays, check identification numbers, and collect vehicle import duties.












Electronics

Purple · Chip icon

Phones, computers, and high-value devices. Use specialist scanners and serial-number checks. Incorrect declarations trigger a manual review.












Food and agriculture

Orange · Apple icon

Produce, plants, and animal products. Add temperature checks, quarantine areas, and time-sensitive processing.












Regulated and hazardous goods

Red · Warning icon

Special handling, safety checks, and dedicated storage. More complex cargo earns greater rewards but needs better facilities.












High-value and priority shipments

Gold · Diamond icon

Luxury goods and urgent deliveries. These reward fast, accurate handling and can unlock premium processing contracts.












Mail and parcels

Grey · Envelope icon

Large volumes of small packages. This category is ideal for automated sorting belts and high-throughput processing.



I'd introduce these gradually rather than show all seven at the start. The cargo category determines the workflow, while the specific shipment provides variety within that category.
3. The shipment should have a visible journey
Every shipment moves through a small number of clearly defined states.
Arrived

Cargo enters the terminal

Declaration checked

Cargo and paperwork are matched

Inspected

Routine scan or manual inspection

Cleared

Duty paid; shipment released


Flagged

Additional review required



Flagged shipments return to the inspection workflow. Incorrect or prohibited cargo can be held or seized.

This creates natural gameplay decisions. Do you send an ordinary package straight through a fast lane, or use the slower scanner? Do you invest in a second inspection bay, or hire an inspector? Does a suspicious shipment justify delaying the queue?

The player doesn't need to learn customs law. These can be simplified game rules with clear visual feedback.
4. Progression: from border booth to international hub









Stage 1: Border checkpoint

One arrival lane, one desk, one scanner, and general cargo. You carry paperwork and packages manually.











Stage 2: Regional cargo terminal

Hire your first staff, add inspection lanes, and unlock electronics and vehicle imports.











Stage 3: Major logistics hub

Add automated conveyors, refrigerated storage, specialist inspectors, and multiple warehouses.











Stage 4: International trade port

Handle shipping containers, high-volume truck traffic, priority cargo, and more complex declarations.











Stage 5: National customs authority

Operate several terminals, manage trade routes, and allocate budgets across airports, seaports, and land borders.



I'd make each new stage introduce a new type of gameplay, not merely larger numbers. A port introduces containers and cranes; an airport introduces luggage and time-sensitive flights; a land border introduces queues of vehicles.
5. Staff and automation
Workers should have clear jobs and physical behaviours. That makes the terminal feel alive and gives upgrades a visible effect.
Staff role	What they do
Cargo handler	Moves shipments between stations
Document clerk	Checks declarations and paperwork
Scanner operator	Processes routine cargo
Inspector	Handles flagged or complex shipments
Duty officer	Calculates charges and completes clearance
Supervisor	Improves nearby workers' productivity

The player begins by doing all of these jobs. Hiring someone should remove a specific chore from the player's workload.

For example, hiring a cargo handler means you no longer need to carry every crate to the scanner. Upgrading the scanner increases its throughput. Hiring an inspector clears the queue of flagged shipments.

The key is that automation should be visible. You should be able to stand in the middle of your terminal and watch cargo flow through a system you built.
6. Monetization that fits the game
I'd focus on four options:
- Rewarded ads: Double the reward from a completed shipment batch, or get a temporary processing-speed boost.
- Permanent upgrades: Faster scanners, additional lanes, better conveyor belts, and expanded storage.
- Premium expansions: Optional terminal wings, special cargo facilities, or visual themes.
- Remove ads: A one-time purchase that removes forced ads while leaving optional rewarded ads available.
Avoid making players pay to clear routine queues or forcing them to watch ads whenever cargo gets delayed. The fun should come from improving the operation, not escaping artificial frustration.
7. What I'd actually build first
For a first playable prototype, I'd keep the scope small:
- One border checkpoint with one arrival lane.
- Three cargo types: general parcels, electronics, and vehicles.
- Three stations: document desk, scanner, and clearance counter.
- One currency earned from successfully cleared shipments.
- Two staff roles: cargo handler and document clerk.
- Five or six upgrades that improve speed, capacity, or automation.
- A short progression path that unlocks a second lane.
That is enough to test the central question: is carrying, routing, inspecting, and clearing cargo satisfying before the game becomes a large management simulator?

If that first loop feels good, the rest of the game can grow around it. If it doesn't, adding airports, ports, dozens of cargo types, and complex systems won't fix the underlying problem.
